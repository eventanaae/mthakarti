Grammar: Adverbs of Degree

The idea in detail

Adverbs of degree tell us how much or to what extent. They usually go before the adjective or adverb they modify: very tired, quite quickly, extremely important. They make your English more exact: "It was warm" is different from "It was extremely hot".

StrengthAdverbsExample
Very strongextremely, completely, absolutely, totallyThe pilot was extremely calm.
Strongvery, really, highly, soThe story was really informative.
Mediumquite, fairly, rather, prettyThe film was fairly long.
Weaka little, slightly, a bitI felt slightly nervous.
Almost nothardly, barely, scarcelyHe could hardly speak.

Gradable and non-gradable adjectives

This is the key idea in the exam. Gradable adjectives can have levels: cold (a little cold, very cold). They take very, quite, slightly, rather. Non-gradable (extreme or absolute) adjectives are already at the top: freezing, exhausted, perfect, impossible, terrified, huge. They take absolutely, completely, totally, utterly, but NOT "very".

GradableNon-gradable
very tiredabsolutely exhausted
very coldabsolutely freezing
very goodabsolutely perfect
very frightenedcompletely terrified

Enough and too

enough comes after an adjective: "safe enough", "old enough". But it comes before a noun: "enough time". too means "more than is good" and gives a negative result: "It is too dangerous (so we cannot go)". very only intensifies: "It is very hot (but we can still go)".

Example 1. "After the long flight, the passengers were ____ exhausted." Options: very / absolutely / quite. Exhausted is non-gradable → absolutely.
Example 2. "The film was ____ long, but not too long." Medium level → fairly / quite.
Example 3. "The water is not warm ____ to swim." Enough goes after the adjective → enough (warm enough).
Example 4. "The room was so noisy that I could ____ hear the teacher." Almost not → hardly.
⚠️ Common mistakes: (1) "very exhausted" ✗ → "absolutely exhausted" ✓. (2) "enough safe" ✗ → "safe enough" ✓. (3) too is not very: "too good" suggests a problem. (4) "hardly" is not "hard": "He works hard" ≠ "He hardly works". (5) Using "much" before adjectives: "much tired" ✗ → "very tired" ✓.
Exam corner: G.2.8 Adverbs (degree), Part 2. When the options are very, absolutely and slightly, check the adjective after the gap: gradable or non-gradable? Then check the meaning: strong, weak or almost none.

Quick summary

  • Degree adverbs go before adjectives and adverbs.
  • Strength scale: extremely > very > quite > slightly > hardly.
  • Gradable → very/quite; non-gradable → absolutely/completely.
  • enough after adjective; too = negative result.
