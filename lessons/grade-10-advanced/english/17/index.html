Reading 3A: Medical Advice and the Internet

The idea in detail

This theme is about using the internet for health advice. It is very relevant today: many people type their symptoms into a search engine instead of seeing a doctor. The text explains both sides: the internet is convenient (fast, free, instant) but not always reliable (trustworthy).

Main points of the topic

  • Advantages: instant answers, saves time, easy to reach.
  • Risks: some websites are fraudulent and sell "miracle cures" to make money. Self-diagnosis (deciding for yourself what illness you have) can be dangerous because the same symptoms can have many causes.
  • Expert opinion: the internet is a useful starting point, but never a replacement for a doctor.
  • How to check a source: who wrote it (expert?), does it give evidence, when was it updated, and what is its purpose (to inform or to sell)? Reliable sites often end in .gov or .edu or belong to hospitals.

How the text is built and how to read it

The text uses a problem-solution structure: it starts with a common habit, then says "However" and introduces the problem, then gives a quotation from an expert, and ends with advice. Signal words: however, but, although, because. A quotation from an expert (here Dr. Salem) usually carries the writer's main message, so it is a good place to look for the answer to inference and overall-meaning questions.

Model analysis (word in context). "Some pages are written by experts, but others are fraudulent, promoting dishonest 'miracle cures'." The word but shows a contrast: experts vs fraudulent. So "reliable" in the nearby sentence is the opposite of fraudulent → "can be trusted".
Model answer (detail). Question: "What should you check before you trust a health website?" Answer in the text: who wrote it, whether it gives evidence, and when it was updated. A correct option will say something like "the author and the date", not "how many people visit it".
Model inference. "Doctors warn that self-diagnosis can be dangerous." What would a doctor probably advise? That you see a doctor if symptoms continue. This is supported by "never a replacement for a doctor".
⚠️ Common mistakes: (1) Choosing "popular" or "fast" as the meaning of reliable. (2) Believing that every website with a medical word is correct. (3) Selecting an option that says "the internet is dangerous and should never be used"; the text says it is a useful starting point (too extreme). (4) Not noticing contrast words (however, but).
Exam corner: Part 3A theme: A Picture of Health | Medical advice and the Internet. 3 marks per question. For the "main idea" question, choose the option that includes both sides: useful but needs checking. For vocabulary, the words symptoms, fraudulent and dishonest from Part 1 also appear here, so reading them in context helps in both parts.

Quick summary

  • Internet health advice: fast and convenient but not always reliable.
  • Fraudulent sites sell fake cures; self-diagnosis is risky.
  • Check: author, evidence, date, purpose.
  • Use the internet as a start, then see a doctor.
