Grammar 3: Adverbs of Quantity

📘 اللغة الإنجليزية🎓 الصف الثاني عشر متقدم📌 هيكل الامتحان: Part 2 · Adverbs of quantity / degree

🎯 ذاكر الدرس وحل الكويز مجانًا في مذاكرتي

Adverbs (and expressions) of quantity tell us how much or to what degree. They often come before an adjective or adverb, or after a verb.

ExpressionMeaningExample (living roofs)
a lot / mucha large amount (much: mainly negatives, questions, before comparatives)Living roofs help a lot. / They are much cooler.
a little / a bit / slightlya small amountThe plants need a little water in summer.
little (no 'a')almost none (negative idea)Sedum plants need little care.
hardly / barelyalmost notA living roof hardly needs any maintenance.
too + adjectivemore than is goodThe roof was too weak to hold the soil.
adjective + enoughas much as necessaryIs the roof strong enough?
quite / fairly / rathermoderatelyInstallation is quite expensive.
almost / nearlynot completelyThe roof absorbs almost all the rainwater.
completely / totally / extremelyto the maximum / veryThe garden is completely self-watering.

Also for nouns: many / few / a few + countable (many plants, a few insects); much / little / a little + uncountable (much water, little noise).

Position: enough comes AFTER an adjective (strong enough) but BEFORE a noun (enough soil). too comes before the adjective (too heavy).
⚠️ 'a little' = some (positive); 'little' = almost none (negative). 'hardly' already has a negative meaning: 'It hardly needs any water', not 'It doesn't hardly need'.

The idea in detail

Adverbs of quantity (also called adverbs of degree) show how much or how strongly. They change the strength of an adjective, an adverb or a verb. Quantifiers such as many, much, few, little go before nouns.

Small, medium and large amounts

AmountWordsExample
none / almost nonehardly, barely, little, fewThe roof hardly needs any water.
smalla little, a bit, slightly, a fewThe plants need a little water.
mediumquite, fairly, ratherInstallation is quite expensive.
almost allalmost, nearlyIt absorbs almost all the rain.
very large / totalvery, extremely, completely, totallyThe roof is completely self-watering.
more than neededtooThe roof is too weak.
as much as neededenoughThe roof is strong enough.

Why / how it works

Countable or uncountable? We can count plants, insects, roofs (use many, few, a few, fewer). We cannot count water, soil, noise (use much, little, a little, less). "A lot of" works with both.

"A" changes the meaning. a few / a little = some, positive ("We have a little time - let's start"). few / little (no a) = almost none, negative ("We have little time - hurry!"). The "a" turns the glass half full.

Position. Too and very go before an adjective: "too heavy". Enough goes after an adjective ("strong enough") but before a noun ("enough soil"). Hardly goes before the main verb ("hardly needs") or before "any / ever".

Too vs very. "Very" is just strong ("The roof is very heavy"); "too" shows a problem ("The roof is too heavy to support the soil"). Too + adjective + (for someone) + to + verb is a common exam pattern.

Worked example 1

Choose the correct word: "Sedum plants need ____ care." Options: few / little / a few.
"care" is uncountable → few and a few are out. "Sedum plants are popular because they almost look after themselves", so the idea is negative (almost no care) → little.

Worked example 2

Complete the sentences.
a) "The old roof was ____ weak to carry wet soil." (a problem → more than is good) → too.
b) "Is the roof strong ____?" (after an adjective) → enough.
c) "Living roofs ____ need maintenance once the plants have grown." (almost no) → hardly.
d) "The soil absorbs ____ all the rainwater." (not completely) → almost.
e) "How ____ water does the roof need?" (uncountable) → much.

Common mistakes

⚠️ 1) "enough strong" ✗ → "strong enough" ✓. 2) "doesn't hardly" ✗ - hardly is already negative; use "hardly needs" or "doesn't need". 3) Confusing a little (some) with little (almost none). 4) Using "many" with uncountable nouns (many water ✗, much water ✓). 5) "very too heavy" ✗: just "too heavy". 6) "much" in positive statements: use "a lot of" ("They have a lot of plants", not "much plants").

Exam corner

How it is asked: "Part 2 - Adverbs of quantity / degree." Gap sentences about living roofs with options such as hardly / much / too / enough. Full marks: (1) check what follows the gap (adjective, noun, verb); (2) countable or uncountable? (3) is the idea positive or negative? (4) check position (enough after the adjective); (5) reread the whole sentence.

Quick summary

  • Little/few (almost none) vs a little/a few (some).
  • many/few + countable; much/little + uncountable; a lot of = both.
  • too + adjective (problem); adjective + enough (sufficient).
  • hardly/barely = almost not (no second negative).
  • almost/nearly = not completely; completely/totally = 100%.
