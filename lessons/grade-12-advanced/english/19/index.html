Course Skills: Convincing, Persuading & Conditionals

The idea in detail

This lesson is from Unit 1 (Listening and Speaking, pp. 17-19). It teaches you the difference between convincing and persuading, the language for each, and how to use conditionals to be persuasive.

  • Convince = make someone believe that something is true or that a problem exists. Grammar: convince someone that ... / of something.
  • Persuade = make someone decide to do something. Grammar: persuade someone to do something.

Example: "The video convinced me that plastic is a problem, and the speaker persuaded me to join the beach clean-up."

Why / how it works

Two steps. A speaker usually first convinces the audience (shows the problem and its importance) and then persuades them (gives a clear action). If the audience does not believe the problem exists, they will never act.

Conditionals for persuasion.

  • First conditional (real future possibility): If + present simple, will + verb: "If we don't reduce plastic waste, our oceans will become more polluted."
  • Unless = if ... not: "Unless we act now, nothing will change." (= If we don't act now, nothing will change.)
  • Zero conditional for facts: "If plastic enters the sea, it harms marine animals."

Persuasive techniques: rhetorical question ("Do we want to leave our children a polluted planet?"), personal pronouns (we, you, our - they create unity), repetition, emotive language (shocking, devastating, precious), the rule of three ("reduce, reuse, recycle"), hyperbole (strong exaggeration), and conditionals.

Idiom: to twist someone's arm = to persuade someone to do something they do not want to do. ("I didn't want to go, but my friends twisted my arm.")

Worked example 1

Short persuasive speech (about water waste):
"What many people fail to realise is that every drop counts. If we leave the tap running while we brush our teeth, we waste litres of water every day. Unless we change our habits now, our water supply will be in danger. So what can you do? First, turn off the tap. Second, report leaks. Third, tell your friends. Reduce, reuse, recycle - it is that simple."
Analysis: convincing phrase ("What many people fail to realise"); conditionals ("If we leave the tap running, we waste..." is a zero conditional about a general fact; "Unless we change... will be in danger" is a first conditional warning); persuading language ("what can you do", steps); rule of three (First, Second, Third; reduce, reuse, recycle); pronouns we/you.

Worked example 2

Correct the sentences.
a) "Unless we don't act, nothing will change." ✗ → Unless we act, nothing will change. (unless already has a negative meaning)
b) "She convinced me to recycle." (this should be persuade) → She persuaded me to recycle. or She convinced me that recycling is important.
c) "If we will reduce plastic, the oceans will be cleaner." ✗ → If we reduce plastic, the oceans will be cleaner.

Common mistakes

⚠️ 1) "will" in the if/unless clause. 2) Double negative with unless. 3) Using persuade + that or convince + to: persuade ... to, convince ... that. 4) Being too aggressive: strong language should still be polite and respectful. 5) Giving only a problem and no action (that is convincing but not persuading).

Exam corner

How it is asked: "Unit 1 - L&S pp. 17-19 (teacher lessons)." These skills support your writing (persuasive language, conditionals) and grammar questions (if/unless, persuade/convince). They are also useful in class speaking tasks. Full marks: state the problem, explain why it matters, give a clear action, and use a mix of techniques and correct conditionals.

Quick summary

  • Convince = believe (that); persuade = act (to).
  • First conditional: If + present, will + verb; unless = if not.
  • Techniques: rhetorical question, pronouns, repetition, emotive language, rule of three, hyperbole.
  • Idiom: twist someone's arm = force them to agree.
