The idea in detail

Part 3A of the exam is an argumentative text called Personality: Nature or Nurture? To understand it easily you need background knowledge about the brain and about the debate. This lesson gives you that background from the course reading The Human Brain.

1. The brain in brief

  • The neocortex ("new brain") is the outer layer used for complex thinking: language, planning, decisions. The subcortex (under it) controls basic functions such as breathing and heartbeat.
  • Left brain / right brain: the popular idea that the left side is logical and the right side creative is now called a myth (a belief that is not true). Most activities use both hemispheres (the two halves) together.
  • Memory is stored in different parts of the brain, and some memories can be false. Meditation may improve memory; "infomania" (constant checking of messages and information) reduces focus.
  • The teenage brain is still maturing: the corpus callosum (the bridge between the two hemispheres) keeps growing into the 20s. This may help explain why teenagers make quick, risky decisions.
  • The brain is plastic (neuroplasticity): it can change and make new connections throughout life.

2. The debate

Meaninggenes inherited from parentsenvironment: family, culture, education, friends, experiences
Key wordheritableplastic brain, upbringing
Evidencetwin studieschanges in people who move to a new culture or get new experiences

Why / how it works

Twin studies: Identical twins share almost all their genes; non-identical twins share about half. If identical twins are more alike in personality than non-identical twins, that supports nature. But twins also often share a home, so nurture cannot be ruled out. This is why scientists say both matter.

Reading signals. In argumentative texts you must separate three things:

  • Fact: research shows, studies provide evidence, it is known that.
  • Assumption / possibility: may, might, probably, suggests, it is believed, scientists theorise.
  • Opinion: I think, in my view, should, the best way.

Worked example 1

Classify: (a) "Studies provide evidence that the brain continues to change in adulthood." → fact (evidence is mentioned). (b) "Meditation may improve memory." → assumption ("may"). (c) "Schools should teach students how their brains work." → opinion ("should").

Worked example 2

Question: "According to the reading, why might teenagers make less careful decisions?" Think: the text says the teenage brain is still maturing and the corpus callosum continues to grow into the 20s. Answer: because the teenage brain is not fully developed yet. Wrong options usually say "teenagers have no neocortex" or "they use only one hemisphere" - these are not in the text.

Common mistakes

⚠️ 1) Mixing what the writer reports (other people's views) with what the writer believes (usually in the last paragraph, "In my view..."). 2) Treating "may" statements as proven facts. 3) Believing the left-brain/right-brain myth. 4) Using "plastic" in the material sense: here it means "able to change". 5) Thinking nature and nurture exclude each other: many texts argue that both work together.

Exam corner

How it is asked: "Part 3A preparation - R&W Unit 1 pp. 4-10." Background knowledge helps with vocabulary-in-context and inference questions in the argumentative text. Full marks: underline words like "may" and "suggest" to detect caution; look at the first and last paragraph for the writer's position; do not pick an option that is "too strong" (always, never, only).

Quick summary

  • Neocortex = complex thinking; subcortex = basic functions.
  • Left-brain/right-brain = myth; both sides work together.
  • The teenage brain is still maturing; the brain is plastic (can change).
  • Nature = genes (heritable); nurture = environment and experience.
  • Learn the signals: fact (research shows), assumption (may, might), opinion (I think, should).
