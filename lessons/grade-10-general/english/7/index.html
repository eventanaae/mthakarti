Listening: Vocal Cues & Body Language

The idea in detail

People do not communicate only with words. In Listening 3, "Did you say that?", you listen to short conversations, for example two students in a crowded cafeteria, and decide how the speakers feel. We show feelings with two things: our voice (vocal cues) and our body (body language).

Vocal cues (الإشارات الصوتية)

  • Tone: the "colour" of the voice, such as warm, sharp, flat.
  • Intonation: the rise and fall of the voice. Rising often means a question or surprise; falling often means a statement, sadness or boredom.
  • Stress: the louder, stronger word shows what is important or emotional.
  • Speed and volume: fast and loud = excited or angry; slow and quiet = sad, tired or bored.

Body language

Shrugging the shoulders (confused or "I don't know"), looking at a watch (impatient), smiling and wide eyes (happy or surprised), crossed arms (angry or closed), hand like a phone at the ear ("call me").

CueFeeling
Shrug, rising "Um...?"confused

Why the voice matters

The same sentence can have opposite meanings. "Great" can be real happiness or sarcasm (سخرية). The listener must compare the words and the voice. If they do not match, the voice usually shows the true feeling.

Example 1. A student gets a present and says slowly, in a low flat voice, "Oh... thanks." The words are polite, but the voice is low and flat, so the feeling is disappointed.
Example 2. Outside, it starts to rain on the day of a picnic. A friend says, "Great... more rain," slowly and flat. The meaning is the opposite: he is not happy. This is sarcasm. If the same words were said with a high, quick, smiling voice, he would be happy because he likes rain.
⚠️ (1) Do not choose the feeling only from the words ("great" = happy). (2) One cue is not always enough: a loud voice can be angry or excited, so use the situation as well. (3) A rising tone does not always mean a question: it can mean surprise. (4) Cultural differences exist: gestures can mean different things in different countries.
Exam corner. In Reading, "inference" questions test a similar skill: you decide what a character feels or what a writer means without a direct statement. Use the clues (words, actions, description). In your speaking and writing about feelings, use adjectives such as confused, annoyed, disappointed, excited, surprised, with a reason: "She felt annoyed because he was late."

Quick summary

  • Feelings are shown by voice (tone, stress, intonation, speed) and body language.
  • Rising tone = question or surprise; low, flat, slow = sad or disappointed.
  • Words and voice may disagree (sarcasm): trust the voice.
  • Vocabulary: confused, annoyed, disappointed, excited, surprised.
